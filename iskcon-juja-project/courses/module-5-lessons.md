## Lesson 1: Is ISKCON Hinduism?

This is the first question many people ask, so we answer it first.

**The short answer.** Most people file ISKCON under Hinduism, and that is understandable: its scriptures (the *Bhagavad-gītā* and *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam*) come from India, and it worships Krishna. But the full answer needs a closer look at two words that are often mixed up: *Hinduism* and *Sanātana-dharma*.

**What "Hinduism" means.** Hinduism is a very large family of traditions. It includes Shaiva, Shakta, Smarta and Vaishnava paths, philosophies that see God as a person and others that see God as impersonal, village customs and great temple traditions, and thousands of local practices. Each has sincere followers. The word "Hindu" comes from Sindhu, the old name of the Indus River, so it began as a name for a place and its people.

**What "Sanātana-dharma" is usually taken to mean.** Many Hindus, and many others, use *Sanātana-dharma* as another word for Hinduism, meaning the practices of Hinduism. But the words themselves mean something much deeper than a set of practices. Let us break the name down.

- **Sanātana** means eternal, without beginning or end.
- **Dharma** means the nature or characteristic of something, that which is intrinsic to it and sustains its existence, without which it would no longer be the same thing.

> We have purposely denoted dharma as occupation because the root meaning of the word dharma is "that which sustains one's existence."
> — *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 1.2.6, purport

The dharma of fire is heat and light; remove them and it is no longer fire. The dharma of water is to flow. The dharma of sugar is sweetness. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

So *Sanātana-dharma* literally means "the eternal nature" of something. Whose nature? The living being's. As Module 4 explained, the soul is an eternal part of God, and the natural function of a part is to serve the whole, as the hand serves the body. The soul's eternal nature, then, is loving service to God. The *Bhāgavatam* says the same in its first chapter:

> The supreme occupation [dharma] for all humanity is that by which men can attain to loving devotional service unto the transcendent Lord. Such devotional service must be unmotivated and uninterrupted to completely satisfy the self.
> — *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 1.2.6

**Two meanings side by side**

| | "Hinduism" (the name) | Sanātana-dharma (the meaning of the words) |
|---|---|---|
| Where the word comes from | A river and a region: the Indus (Sindhu) | The nature of the soul: eternal, without beginning or end |
| Whom it concerns | A particular culture, region and community | Every living being, in every species |
| Can it be joined or left? | A person can adopt or leave a tradition | A soul can forget its nature but cannot lose it |

**Conclusion.** So, is ISKCON Hinduism? In the cultural sense, many people will always classify it that way, and no disrespect is meant to the many Hindu traditions. But what Śrīla Prabhupāda presented is the eternal dharma of the soul: loving service to Krishna. That is not tied to a region, a community or a birth. This is why ISKCON does not present itself as one more sect among many, and why a person from any background can take it up.

ISKCON is an organisation whose purpose is to help everyone awaken their own eternal nature, which is love and service to God. Its aim is not to enrol people in yet another sectarian identity.

## Lesson 2: Is ISKCON a cult?

Two worries often go together. One is "Is this a new group built around one person?" The other is "Does it control people's minds?" This lesson answers both, because both depend on the same question: what is actually being taught, and who needs cleansing?

**What does "cult" mean?** In everyday speech, a cult is a group built around one person's new ideas, with secrecy and pressure on members. Let us test ISKCON against those marks:
- **New ideas?** Its teaching is the *Bhagavad-gītā* and *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam*, which are old, public and open to anyone.
- **Secrecy?** The books are printed and sold openly in many languages. Anyone may read them and ask questions.
- **One person's invention?** Śrīla Prabhupāda presented himself as a servant passing on what he had received.

ISKCON teaches that it belongs to the Brahmā–Madhva–Gauḍīya line of teachers, which comes through Lord Caitanya (who appeared in 1486) from Krishna. Prabhupāda travelled to the West in 1965 and founded ISKCON in New York in 1966. The institution is young in the West; the teaching is not. *(See Modules 1 and 2.)*

> This Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement is not a new movement. It is the oldest. From historical point of view, at least five thousand years old.
> — Lecture, Visakhapatnam, February 18, 1972

> We have not invented something, but we are preaching Bhagavad-gita as it is, without any malinterpretation.
> — Pandal lecture, Bombay, March 31, 1971

No community is free from human mistakes. The better question is whether the teaching is genuine and open to examination. You are invited to examine it.

**Is it brainwashing?** Brainwashing, in the ordinary sense, means forcing a change of mind through deception, isolation, pressure or punishment for asking questions, so that a person loses the freedom to think for himself. Krishna consciousness works the other way. The *Gītā* itself recommends *paripraśna*, sincere inquiry, as part of learning:

> Just try to learn the truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him submissively and render service unto him. The self-realized souls can impart knowledge unto you because they have seen the truth.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 4.34

Newcomers are welcome to read first, ask questions, and go slowly. Prabhupāda himself told people not to rush:

> In whatever position you are, either you are a student or a lawyer or something else, you can chant Hare Kṛṣṇa and realize yourself. We don't recommend that you change your position. That is not our recommendation. But if you can (be) fully devoted in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, that is very nice. But don't do it whimsically. There will be a mature platform when you can do that.
> — Lecture at Harvard University, Boston, December 24, 1969

**Who is being washed?** Here is the part that is usually missed. Nobody's mind is empty. Everyone's mind is already being filled, every day, by advertising, media, peer pressure and the culture around us. We are told that we are our bodies, that happiness is what we own and consume, and that we are defined by nation, tribe or income. Nobody asked our permission. Prabhupāda's point was that this is the real "brainwashing", and that it leaves the mind dirty with confusion and wrong ideas:

> No transformation. The consciousness is there. It is now filled with all rubbish things. You have to cleanse this, and then Kṛṣṇa consciousness… Just like water. Water is, by nature, clear, transparent. But when it is filled up with rubbish things, it is muddy. You cannot see very clearly. But if you filter it, all muddy things, dirty things, then again comes to the original position, clear, transparent water.
> — Room conversation with Sandy Nixon, Philadelphia, July 13, 1975

When critics used the word "brainwash" against the movement, Prabhupāda took the word and turned it around: yes, the brain can be washed, and the question is what it is washed of.

> It is not brainwash; it is brain-clearing. Ceto-darpaṇa-mārjanaṁ bhava-mahā-dāvāgni (CC Antya 20.12). If you want to end this suffering, then you must wash your brain—or heart, the same thing.
> — Lecture on *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 5.5.15, Vṛndāvana, November 3, 1976

In a conversation in January 1977, he told his disciples how to answer such a charge in court:

> Yes. And all these rubbish things we are finishing. "Yes, it is brainwashing, ceto-darpaṇa-mārjanam (CC Antya 20.12), cleansing the core of the heart, all mistaken ideas." Speak in the court like that.
> — Room conversation, Bhuvaneśvara, January 21, 1977

*Ceto-darpaṇa-mārjanam* means cleaning the mirror of the heart. Nothing new is added to the water; the mud is filtered out and the water returns to its natural clarity. So the washing is of the *existing* conditioning, and it is done openly and by invitation. It is voluntary.

**Why people stay.** Nobody is held in Krishna consciousness. People begin out of curiosity or a need, and they continue because they see the results in their own lives: a calmer mind, cleaner habits, more patience with family, freedom from addictions, and a clearer purpose. That is the real reason they stay. A person who observes with neutral eyes, looking at the lives of those who practise sincerely, also sees the benefit. The *Gītā*'s description of this knowledge says as much:

> This knowledge is the king of education, the most secret of all secrets. It is the purest knowledge, and because it gives direct perception of the self by realization, it is the perfection of religion. It is everlasting, and it is joyfully performed.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 9.2

The invitation is to try it and judge by the results, not to take anyone's word for it.

**Conclusion.** Is ISKCON a cult? Not by the usual tests: the teaching is old, open and scriptural, and it does not rest on one person's invention. Is it brainwashing? Everyone's mind is shaped by something. ISKCON's process is voluntary and open, and it clears away layers of conditioning so a person can see clearly and choose for himself. People stay because they see the benefit in their own lives, and anyone looking with neutral eyes can see it too.

## Lesson 3: Which God do you worship? Do you worship many Gods?

**Short answer:** One. There is only one God, and everything else is under Him.

### Can there be more than one God?

Think about what the word "God" means. God means the supreme being: the one who has no equal, above whom there is no other, who is the cause of all causes, the beginning and the end of everything. Now ask: how many beings can fit that description?

Only one. If there were two, neither could be above the other, so neither would be supreme. If one had a cause behind him, he would not be the cause of all causes. By definition, there cannot be two supreme beings, just as there cannot be two things that are each the tallest of everything. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

Our scriptures describe this one God in exactly these terms:

> Kṛṣṇa, who is known as Govinda, is the supreme controller. He has an eternal, blissful, spiritual body. He is the origin of all. He has no other origin, for He is the prime cause of all causes.
> — *Brahma-saṁhitā* 5.1, as quoted in *Caitanya-caritāmṛta*, Madhya 8.137

It follows that there cannot be a "Christian God", a "Muslim God" and a "Hindu God" as three separate beings. There is one God, whom different traditions approach, name and describe in different ways. Prabhupāda put it this way:

> We are preaching God consciousness. God is God. God is neither Christian, nor Hindu, nor Muslim. God is God. So everyone should be God conscious. That is our movement.
> — Room conversation, London, August 14, 1971

So which God do we worship? The one God, whom we know by the name Krishna, which means the all-attractive. *(See Module 1, "Why Krishna consciousness and not just God consciousness?")*

### Then who are Indra, Agni and the other demigods?

They are real and powerful, but they are living beings, not Gods. They are administrators appointed to manage natural functions in the universe, such as rain, fire and wind.

Imagine a company with one chief executive. The heads of finance, operations and human resources each look after a department and have real authority, but they all work under the chief executive. Asking a department head for something is not foolish, but it is a smaller request than going to the one who holds the whole organisation. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

Worship of demigods is respected as a sincere, lower stage of faith. The *Gītā* does not condemn those worshippers; it speaks about the *results* they receive.

> Those whose minds are distorted by material desires surrender unto demigods and follow the particular rules and regulations of worship according to their own natures.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 7.20

> Men of small intelligence worship the demigods, and their fruits are limited and temporary. Those who worship the demigods go to the planets of the demigods, but My devotees ultimately reach My supreme planet.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 7.23

### If there is only one God, why do religions describe Him so differently?

Because God is unlimited. He has no end to His qualities, His forms or His relationships, so no single person, tradition or book can describe all of Him. Our scriptures say so:

> Ananta, endless. You cannot count how many forms are there of the Supreme Personality of Godhead.
> — Lecture on *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 2.3.22, Los Angeles, June 19, 1972

> I worship Govinda, the primeval Lord, who is inaccessible to the Vedas, but obtainable by pure unalloyed devotion of the soul, who is without a second, who is not subject to decay, is without a beginning, whose form is endless, who is the beginning, and the eternal puruṣa; yet He is a person possessing the beauty of blooming youth.
> — *Brahma-saṁhitā* 5.33

Because He is unlimited, He can enter into unlimited kinds of relationships with an unlimited number of living beings: as master, as friend, as parent, as beloved, and in ways we have not even named. This is why sincere people describe Him so differently.

An old story shows how this works. Several blind men are brought to an elephant, an elephant so large that none of them can take in more than a small part. One touches a leg and says, "An elephant is like a tree trunk." Another touches the trunk and says, "No, it is a snake." A third touches an ear and says, "It is a fan." Each has felt something real. Each is right about his part. Each is wrong only if he says the whole elephant is just that.

Notice that nobody in the story is lying. They need two things. First, they need to listen to each other, so that the leg, the trunk and the ear can be put together into a fuller picture. Second, and best of all, they need to listen to someone who can see the whole elephant. And if the elephant is unlimited, even all of them together will never finish touching it, so they will always have more to learn. In the same way, sincere traditions can learn from each other, and the fullest understanding comes from the one who has the complete view: God Himself, and those who hear from Him. *(The story is traditional; it is used here as an illustration.)*

Prabhupāda also used this story, to show that limited senses and speculation alone cannot grasp the whole. The *Bhāgavatam* describes the same truth being realised in different ways:

> Learned transcendentalists who know the Absolute Truth call this nondual substance Brahman, Paramātmā or Bhagavān.
> — *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 1.2.11

> So, the Absolute Truth is realized in three different features, according to the capacity of realization of the person.
> — Lecture on *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 1.2.11, Vṛndāvana, October 22, 1972

And God responds to each person's approach:

> As all surrender unto Me, I reward them accordingly. Everyone follows My path in all respects, O son of Pṛthā.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 4.11

> Everyone is searching for Kṛṣṇa in the different aspects of His manifestations.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 4.11, purport

### Why do religions differ in their rules and customs?

God speaks to people in ways that suit their time, place and ability to receive. The Sanskrit phrase is *deśa-kāla-pātra*: place, time and person. This is why genuine religions differ in many outward details (food rules, rituals, dress, customs) while sharing the same heart: surrender to God and love of God.

> To surrender to God the great and to abide by His instruction, that is called religion. It may be that the Hindus may be following the same principle in a different way or the Christian may be following the same principle in different way. That is called deśa-kāla-pātra. According to time, atmosphere, and the performer, there may be little difference. But real purpose of dharma is to surrender to God and try to love Him. That is religion.
> — Lecture on *Bhagavad-gītā* 7.1, Fiji, May 24, 1975

> Bible begins, "God is the supreme authority," and Bhagavad-gītā concludes, "You surrender." Where is the difference? Simply the description is according to the time, society, and place and people.
> — Lecture, Seattle, October 2, 1968

A pocket dictionary and a large university dictionary are both genuine dictionaries. The small one is written for beginners; the large one gives much more detail. Neither is false. Each serves its reader. Prabhupāda used a similar picture. In the same way, ISKCON respects all bona fide religions and revelations that teach love of God.

> The supreme occupation [dharma] for all humanity is that by which men can attain to loving devotional service unto the transcendent Lord. Such devotional service must be unmotivated and uninterrupted to completely satisfy the self.
> — *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 1.2.6

### Do I have to leave my own faith to use this knowledge?

No. Understanding the soul, the law of karma, how to steady the mind, and how to loosen attachment to material things can help a Christian become a more devoted Christian, a Muslim a more devoted Muslim, and a Hindu a more devoted Hindu. Studying medicine at a university does not make a person belong to one country; the knowledge helps him heal people anywhere. In the same way, spiritual knowledge helps a person love and serve God better within his own tradition. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

**Conclusion.** Which God do we worship? The one God, who by definition cannot be two. Do we worship many Gods? No; the demigods are His administrators. Why do traditions describe Him differently? Like the blind men, each sees a real part, and each can learn from the others and from the one who sees the whole. This is why ISKCON respects every bona fide religion, and invites everyone to use this knowledge to love God more within their own faith.

## Lesson 4: Do you worship idols?

This is often the hardest point for a newcomer, so we take it slowly. The Deity in a temple is not a statue that devotees invented. The Deity (*arcā-vigraha*) is a form of Krishna that He Himself allows, so that we can serve Him personally.

**1. Sanctioned by God, not invented.** Idol worship means making up a form from imagination and then worshipping it. Deity worship follows the scriptures: the materials, proportions, dress, meditation and rituals are laid down in śāstra and handed down through the disciplic line. The *Bhāgavatam* even has a chapter titled "Lord Kṛṣṇa's Instructions on the Process of Deity Worship".

> The Deity form of the Lord is said to appear in eight varieties — stone, wood, metal, earth, paint, sand, the mind or jewels.
> — *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 11.27.12

> Just like we have installed the Deity according to śāstra. There is nothing imaginary. It is not idol worship. Idol worship is different.
> — Lecture on *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 1.7.45–46, Vṛndāvana, October 5, 1976

*Example.* A letter dropped into an official post box reaches its destination because the postal system has authorised that box. A box nailed to a tree by a well-meaning person delivers nothing. The authorised Deity is God's own "post box" for receiving our love. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

**2. God is absolute and all-powerful.** If someone says "God is everywhere, but He cannot appear in a stone, or even in a fly," they have placed a limit on God. If God is truly all-powerful and all-pervading, He can appear in a form of wood, stone or paint without any loss to His majesty.

> By Me, in My unmanifested form, this entire universe is pervaded.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 9.4

> Kṛṣṇa can appear in any way. He is all-powerful. So when He appears just like a stone statue, that does not mean Kṛṣṇa is stone or statue. Kṛṣṇa is the same Kṛṣṇa, but He appears before me just like stone statue because I cannot touch anything beyond this stone. I cannot see beyond this stone. So it is His mercy.
> — Lecture, London, August 17, 1971

*Example.* Electric current is one and the same energy whether it is running in a lamp, a heater or a fan. Prabhupāda used the same picture:

> So everything is Kṛṣṇa's, and from everything, Kṛṣṇa can appear and accept your service. This is the philosophy. He can appear Himself through stone, because stone is His energy. Just like if the electric power is running on, from anywhere you can take electricity, energy.
> — Lecture on *Brahma-saṁhitā* 5.37, London, August 11, 1971

**3. A place to serve Him personally.** Love needs something to do. Someone who says "I love my mother" but never brings her a glass of water has not yet shown it. In our present condition we cannot see or touch Krishna's spiritual form with material senses. Out of mercy, He comes in a form we can bathe, dress, offer food to and decorate. This world is the place where we can practise serving Him personally, and the Deity makes that practice possible.

> Bhakti, or devotional service, means engaging all our senses in the service of the Lord, the Supreme Personality of Godhead, the master of all the senses. When the spirit soul renders service unto the Supreme, there are two side effects. One is freed from all material designations, and, simply by being employed in the service of the Lord, one's senses are purified.
> — *Caitanya-caritāmṛta*, Madhya 19.170 (quoting *Bhakti-rasāmṛta-sindhu* 1.1.12)

> If one offers Me with love and devotion a leaf, a flower, fruit or water, I will accept it.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 9.26

**4. A point of focus for the mind.** The mind needs something to hold on to. Try speaking to "space" for ten minutes and then speak to a friend sitting in front of you; the difference is obvious. The Deity gives the mind a form, a face and a beauty to meditate on, and turns philosophy into a relationship.

> For those whose minds are attached to the unmanifested, impersonal feature of the Supreme, advancement is very troublesome. To make progress in that discipline is always difficult for those who are embodied.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 12.5

> It is simply troublesome, kleśa. Kleśa means troublesome. Because they cannot concentrate.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā* lecture, Montreal, July 20, 1968

## Lesson 5: Do you worship Prabhupāda and gurus?

**A common view:** "Devotees worship Śrīla Prabhupāda or their gurus as if they were God."

**ISKCON's answer:** a genuine guru is never God and never claims to be. He is honoured as the most confidential servant of God. Respect is shown to the messenger because of the message he carries.

> The spiritual master is to be honored as much as the Supreme Lord because he is the most confidential servitor of the Lord. This is acknowledged in all revealed scriptures and followed by all authorities.
> — Śrī Gurv-aṣṭaka 7, as translated in *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 7.5.11, purport

> Then what is his position? Kintu prabhor yaḥ priya eva tasya: he is the most confidential servant of Kṛṣṇa. He never says that "I am Kṛṣṇa, I am God." That is not guru.
> — Lecture on *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* 1.15.25–26, Los Angeles, December 4, 1973

*Example.* An ambassador is received with honours and a red carpet, not because he is the president, but because he carries the president's authority. If an ambassador claimed to be the president, he would be a fraud. A genuine guru always points to Krishna. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

**Gratitude is the root of the respect.** Think about what the guru gives. He offers his life to deliver the most valuable thing there is: knowledge of God and the path to pure love of God. If a doctor saved your life, would you treat him as just another person? Gratitude of this kind is natural and does not have to be forced.

**Why training is sometimes needed.** Because of conditioning and ego, that natural gratitude may be buried. A small child may need to be reminded to say "thank you" before the habit becomes real feeling. In the same way, spiritual practice (*sādhana*) and humility help bring out respect that is already part of the soul. The *Gītā* describes the attitude in which this learning happens:

> Just try to learn the truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him submissively and render service unto him. The self-realized souls can impart knowledge unto you because they have seen the truth.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 4.34

**A note on Prabhupāda and other gurus.** Honour to Śrīla Prabhupāda and to other gurus in the line is honour to the servants of Krishna. It does not replace worship of Krishna, and it is not a claim that any man is God.

## Lesson 6: Do devotees abandon their families and work?

**A common view:** "Joining means leaving your family, quitting your job and begging on the street."

**ISKCON's answer.** Krishna consciousness does not require anyone to walk away from responsibilities. Most people who follow this path live in ordinary homes, work in ordinary jobs (as teachers, doctors, engineers, business owners and so on) and raise families. Bhakti-yoga does not break the engine of your career or family; it changes the direction in which you drive it. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

> In whatever position you are, either you are a student or a lawyer or something else, you can chant Hare Kṛṣṇa and realize yourself. We don't recommend that you change your position.
> — Lecture at Harvard University, Boston, December 24, 1969

> … we do not recommend that "You give up your occupation, you become a sannyasi, give up your wife and children." No. That is not our movement.
> — Lecture, Bombay, September 25, 1973

**Renunciation, under guidance.** Some people do feel called to a life of full-time service as celibate students (*brahmacārī*) or renounced monks (*sannyāsī*). This is allowed for those who are willing and mature enough, and it is done under experienced guidance and within clear limits. Someone may not abandon a young family. A man may not leave a wife unprotected or small children unsupported, even if he sincerely wishes to renounce. Responsibility comes first. Think of a pilot: whatever his personal ambitions, he does not leave the cockpit in mid-flight. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

> It is not advisable in this Age of Kali to leave one's family suddenly, for people are not trained as proper brahmacārīs and gṛhasthas. Therefore Śrī Caitanya Mahāprabhu advised the brāhmaṇa not to be too eager to give up family life.
> — *Caitanya-caritāmṛta*, Madhya 7.127, purport

True renunciation is also about the heart, not only about outward appearance:

> One who restrains the senses of action but whose mind dwells on sense objects certainly deludes himself and is called a pretender.
> — *Bhagavad-gītā As It Is* 3.6

**"Why do some people seem to drift away from their old friends?"** Any big change in lifestyle changes who we spend time with. A person who gives up drinking spends fewer evenings at the bar. A new parent sees less of the old crowd. Someone who takes up marathon running finds new friends at the track. In the same way, a person who takes up a clean, simple, devotional life finds that he naturally has more in common with people who share it. The old proverb says "birds of a feather flock together". This is not isolation and nobody is told to cut anyone off. Real friendships and family ties usually survive and often grow deeper, because the person becomes calmer, kinder and more reliable. And devotees are encouraged to keep treating family and friends with love and respect. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

## Lesson 7: Conclusion: come, learn, and take what you can use

In this module we have looked at the questions people most often ask. Here is each answer in a sentence:

- **Is ISKCON Hinduism?** Many people classify it that way, but what it presents is the eternal dharma of the soul: loving service to God.
- **Is it a cult?** No. Its teaching is old, open and scriptural, and people stay because they see the benefit in their own lives.
- **Which God? Many Gods?** One God, who by definition cannot be two. The demigods are His administrators, and different traditions each describe real parts of Him.
- **Do you worship idols?** No. The Deity is a form that God Himself allows, so that we can serve Him personally.
- **Do you worship Prabhupāda and gurus?** No. They are honoured with gratitude as servants of God.
- **Do devotees abandon family and work?** No. Most live ordinary lives, and renunciation is guided and limited.

### Awakening, not converting

ISKCON has its own religious practices, and those who sincerely wish to take them up formally are welcome. But its main aim in sharing this knowledge is not to change anyone's religious label. It is to help people open their eyes to the truth, whatever religion they come from, because the underlying principles of every bona fide religion are the same: love of God, obedience to God, and service to God. Prabhupāda said it plainly:

> And our process, this Kṛṣṇa consciousness movement, is not for proselytizing, that "You are Christian. You become Hindu," or "You are Hindu. You become Christian," or "You are Muhammadan. You…" No. We don't recommend in that way. You remain in your religious faith, but we simply request you that you develop your love of God. That's all.
> — *Renunciation Through Wisdom* 2.10

> I don't say that "You Christian, you become Hindu or you come to me." I simply say "You obey these commandments." … I don't say that you have to come to this platform and accept Kṛṣṇa as God and no other. No. I don't say. I say, "Please obey God. Please try to love God." That is my mission.
> — Press interview, Los Angeles, December 30, 1968

When a person wakes from sleep, he does not become someone else; he becomes clear. Awakening love of God in a Christian, a Muslim or a Hindu does not erase his roots; it deepens his sincerity. *(An illustration, not a Prabhupāda quotation.)*

### Living together peacefully

When we understand that God is one and not sectarian, three things change in everyday life:

1. **Less sectarian fanaticism.** If God is one and loves all, we can respect sincere people of every faith without feeling threatened by them.
2. **Essence over custom.** We learn to tell the difference between what is essential (love of God, compassion, honesty) and what is regional custom (dress, ritual details). Many disputes between communities are about the second.
3. **Cooperative citizenship.** Devotees can work alongside neighbours of every belief on shared goals: peace, honesty, care for the hungry, spiritual welfare. Food for Life at ISKCON Juja is one example of this in practice.
